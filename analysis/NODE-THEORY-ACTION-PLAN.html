MARK-WHAT-MARK

Section 1 · The business in one page

What Node Theory is

We run a rack of real iPhones in our own space. Clients hand us the accounts they already own, and we operate those accounts on our phones: posting on a schedule, answering messages, watching for problems, and sending back proof that everything landed. They stop thinking about it. That is the product.

Said plainly

A client has ten social accounts they are responsible for. Their own brand pages, their creators' official pages, their clients' pages. Posting to all of them by hand eats a person's whole week. Scheduling tools cover some of it, but not the parts the platforms keep inside the phone app: Stories, the reply inbox, seller tools, anything that needs a real device.

We put each of those accounts on a real iPhone we own and run. We drive the actual app the same way a person would, with taps and typing. The phone does not know it is being driven, because at the hardware level it is talking to what looks like an ordinary keyboard and mouse.

The client sends us content and a calendar. We send back posts, replies, and screenshots proving each one happened.

Tip The sentence to use on a call

"You own the accounts. We own the phones, and we push the buttons on them for you, on schedule, and prove it." Everything else in this document is detail underneath that sentence.

Why real phones instead of software

Three reasons, in order of how much they matter to a buyer.

  • Reach. The official posting APIs do not cover everything. Stories, DMs, seller apps, live shopping tools and most in-app settings are phone-only. A real phone reaches all of it because it is just using the app.
  • Durability. Scheduling tools break when a platform changes its API terms. A phone keeps working because there is nothing to revoke. If the app runs, we can run it.
  • Proof. We can screenshot the actual screen at native resolution. The client sees the post as it appeared on a real device, not a status code from an API.

Whose accounts we run

Only accounts the client owns or is contractually authorized to operate, and we collect that authorization in writing before a phone is assigned. That is not a legal footnote, it is the shape of the business. It decides which customers we can take, what we can charge, whether we can use Stripe, and whether the whole thing is still here in three years.

Section 7 spells out where the line sits and why stating it out loud wins deals rather than losing them.

The one fact that decides what we can sell

The control software has a property that shapes every price in this document.

  • Doing something costs one request, no matter how many phones do it. Tapping the same spot on one phone or on fifty is a single instruction, and the boards carry it out on all of them at the same time. Posting identical content to a whole group is nearly free for us.
  • Checking what happened costs one request per phone. Screenshots come back one at a time. Watching fifty phones costs fifty times watching one.

So the cheap products are the ones where many phones do the same thing: synchronized posting, fleet-wide maintenance, one campaign across a group. The expensive products are the ones where every phone needs its own attention: individual captions, inbox replies, monitoring, proof of every post. We charge accordingly, and we build the cheap half first.

What we are not

We are not a dashboard company. We are not selling software the client logs into and operates themselves. If we ever ship a client-facing app it is a reporting window, not the product. The product is that a machine and a person are looking after their accounts and they do not have to.

That also means our costs are labor-shaped, not code-shaped. Section 5 works through what that does to margin, and Section 6 works through how many devices one operator can actually hold.

MARK-OFFER-MARK

Section 2 · The commercial offer

What we sell

We sell a managed device. One iPhone in our rack, assigned to one of the client's accounts, operated by us on a schedule, with an uptime promise and proof of every action. Everything else is packaging around that unit.

The unit: one managed device

A client buys devices, not software seats and not "phones powered on." A device comes with the account assigned to it, the work we do on it, and the evidence that the work happened. If the device is dark, we are not billing for it.

Two things sold separately

  • Onboarding, $250 per device, one time. Sourcing and preparing the phone, signing in, verifying authorization, calibrating taps for that model, and running the first test post. This is real work and should never be free, because giving it away invites clients who churn in month two.
  • Device lab, priced per project. The same rack rented as a test bench: checking that links, push notifications, emails and checkout flows work on real iPhones. Section 4 explains why this line matters out of proportion to its revenue.

What the client has to do

Keep this list short. Every item on it is a reason a deal stalls.

  1. Sign the authorization form for each account, once.
  2. Give us access to the account, and keep two-factor recovery on their side.
  3. Drop content and captions in a shared folder, with dates.
  4. Answer us when something needs a human decision.

Trap Do not sell "unlimited posting"

One device can physically do a post every 40 to 60 seconds if driven back to back, which sounds like unlimited. It is not, because the platforms themselves start pushing back long before the hardware does, and because every post we have to verify individually costs us a screenshot and an operator glance. Sell a number, cap it, and charge for overage. The cap protects the account as much as it protects our margin.

The promise we make

Keep the service level simple enough to say on a call and to measure without building a whole system for it.

  • Device uptime 99% monthly, measured as the device being reachable and the account signed in. Credit back a day's fee per full day down.
  • Scheduled posts land within 15 minutes of their slot, or we tell the client why before they ask.
  • Problems reported within one business hour of us detecting them.
  • No lock-in. Thirty days' notice, and we hand back the accounts cleanly and sign out of the device in front of them if they want to watch.

That last point is worth more than it looks. The obvious fear with handing accounts to an infrastructure vendor is that leaving is painful. Saying the exit is clean, in writing, removes the main objection in the room.

Module A · Publishing and distribution

This is the revenue engine. Most of it fans out for free, which is why the packages can include generous post counts without hurting margin.

Scheduled posting to the real app

Needs phone helperCore

The client drops a photo or video and a caption into a folder with a date. At that time, the picture lands on the phone, the app opens, the composer is filled in, and it posts. Because it goes through the app itself, it reaches things the official scheduling tools cannot, including Stories and Reels.

How

Push the file to the phone's camera roll, put the caption on the phone's clipboard, then drive the composer with taps and paste it in.

Cost to us

Fans out free when the content is the same across a group. Different captions per account cost one small call each.

Stories, Reels and short vertical video

Needs phone helperCore

The formats that actually get reach. Same pipeline as a feed post, with the extra taps that the Story and Reel composers need. We can also drag a sticker or a link to a set position on the screen before publishing.

Cost to us

Roughly 30 to 45 seconds of device time per Reel, and about 15 seconds per Story, on top of the upload.

One asset, every platform

Needs phone helperCore

Upload the video once to the phone, then send it out to six platforms from the phone's own share sheet, or open each app's composer directly with a link. The client does one upload and gets posts everywhere.

Cost to us

The upload happens once per phone. Each additional platform is under a minute of device time.

The posting calendar

Needs build workCore

The scheduler is ours, not the phone's. A job says what to post, to which group of devices, at what time, and what to do if it fails. This is the piece of software we own, and it is where the service actually lives.

Cost to us

Build once. Running it is nearly free.

Synchronized drop

Needs phone helperAdd-on

Everything publishes at the same moment across every device in the group. Useful for a product launch or a coordinated campaign push where the client wants one visible spike rather than a trickle.

How

Stage every phone up to the final button, then send one instruction that presses publish on all of them together.

Cost to us

One request for the whole fleet. This is the cheapest impressive thing we can demo.

Emoji, accents and non-English captions

Needs phone helperCore

The keyboard path only handles plain English letters and numbers. Anything else, including emoji, accented characters and other alphabets, goes through the phone's clipboard and a paste. Worth naming explicitly on sales calls with anyone posting outside English.

Cost to us

One small call per distinct caption.

Back off when the platform pushes back

Ready nowCore

If the app shows "Action Blocked" or "Try again later", we stop that account, put it on cool-down, and tell the client. This protects the account and it is the honest opposite of pushing through a limit. Say this one out loud in sales conversations.

How

Compare a small crop of the screen against the known shape of those warning dialogs before continuing a run.

Cost to us

One screenshot per phone per check. Cheap, and it runs on a timer, not on every action.

A/B creative split across device groups

Needs phone helperAdd-on

Put creative A on one group of accounts and creative B on another, publish both at once, then collect the in-app numbers a day later and hand back a comparison. Agencies pay for this because it is the thing they are supposed to be doing and rarely have time to do.

Cost to us

Publishing is free to fan out. The results harvest is per phone.

Module B · Inbox and community

This is where the Desk package earns its price, and where our costs are highest, because none of it fans out. Every message is one account's problem.

Inbox watch

Needs build workCore

We check each account regularly for new direct messages, comments and mentions, and raise a flag when something is waiting. The client stops opening ten apps to find out whether anything happened.

How

Look at the app's badge and the notification list, and compare against the last check.

Cost to us

One screenshot per phone per sweep. Nineteen phones once a second is well inside what the rack handles, so cadence is a policy choice, not a technical limit.

One sweep of every notification, without opening apps

Ready nowCore

Pull down the notification shade on every phone at once and photograph it. In one pass we see which accounts have something new and roughly from which app, without touching a single inbox.

Cost to us

The pull-down is one instruction for the whole rack. Only the screenshots are per phone.

Staffed reply desk

Needs phone helperDesk package

An operator sees the live screen of each phone in a browser and answers messages from a queue, with saved replies for the common ones. Replies with emoji go through the clipboard. This is a person doing the work with good tooling, and it should be priced as such.

Cost to us

Operator time is the whole cost. Budget one operator per 40 to 60 conversation-light accounts, far fewer if the accounts are busy.

Comment replies at scale

Needs phone helperAdd-on

Reply to comments under the client's own posts, from the account that made the post. Approved reply text comes from the client, and anything outside the approved set goes to a human.

Cost to us

Per account. Identical replies across a group are one call, but identical replies are usually the wrong answer.

Outbound follow-ups the client is allowed to send

Needs phone helperLater

Order updates, booking confirmations and replies to people who contacted the client first, sent through WhatsApp, Telegram or Messages by opening a pre-filled link and pressing send.

Trap This one carries real legal weight

Unsolicited commercial messages to phone numbers are regulated hard in the US, UK and EU, with per-message penalties. Only ever send to recipients who asked, keep the client's consent records, and put the liability in the contract. If a prospect wants cold outreach volume, decline the work.

Module C · Account health and keeping the lights on

This module is why a client pays a monthly fee instead of buying a script. None of it is glamorous and all of it is the actual product.

Is the account still signed in?

Ready nowCore

On a schedule, bring each phone to the app's home screen and sort what we see into one of four buckets: signed in and normal, signed out, asked to verify, or restricted. The client hears about a problem from us rather than from a missed campaign.

How

Compare a few small regions of the screen against known-good reference pictures. This works today without the text-recognition add-on.

Cost to us

About four seconds of device time per account per check, so a full rack sweep is minutes, not hours.

The two-light health board

Ready nowCore

Each phone shows two independent lights: can we control it, and can we see it. They fail separately, and confusing them wastes hours. A phone can show a perfect picture and ignore every tap, or accept every tap while showing nothing.

Cost to us

Two calls return the state of the whole rack, regardless of size. Effectively free.

Proof that taps are actually landing

Ready nowCore

A phone can quietly stop accepting taps while still looking healthy in every other way. We nudge the pointer a few pixels on a timer and confirm the screen reacts. If it does not, the phone is flagged before a real job runs on it.

Why this matters commercially

This is the single fault most likely to make us miss a client's posts without noticing. It is a measured, real failure on our hardware, and the fix is a one-line reset command.

Self-healing ladder

Needs build workCore

When something breaks, work up a ladder automatically before a human is involved: reset the pointer, release stuck keys, restart the app, restart the phone's connection, restart the board, reboot the phone. Escalate to a person only if the ladder runs out.

Cost to us

Each rung is cheap. This is the feature that decides how many devices one operator can hold, which is the main driver of our margin.

App crash and frozen-screen watchdog

Ready nowCore

Notice when an app has dropped back to the home screen or when the picture has stopped changing, and restart it. Runs quietly at a low rate.

Cost to us

A once-a-second check per phone is a small fraction of what the rack can serve.

Catch a platform redesign before it breaks the schedule

Ready nowAdd-on

Our taps go to fixed positions on the screen. When an app updates and moves a button, every script that touches it breaks. A nightly walk through the key screens, compared against saved reference pictures, tells us which scripts need fixing that morning.

Why this matters commercially

This converts our biggest ongoing maintenance risk into a scheduled morning task, which is the difference between a service and a constant fire.

iOS update tripwire

Needs build workAdd-on

When a phone updates its operating system, tap positions can drift and need recalibrating. We watch the reported version, and when it changes we queue that device for a calibration check before it runs client work again.

Rank the phones by connection quality

Ready nowAdd-on

Two identical phones on the same rack delivered very different picture rates in testing, one about six times slower than the other, purely because of its wireless link. Slow phones take longer to verify and should carry lighter accounts. We score them and place work accordingly.

Why this matters commercially

It stops us from promising the same throughput on every device when the hardware does not deliver it.

Storage cleanup

Needs phone helperAdd-on

Every piece of content we push stays in the phone's camera roll. Left alone, devices fill up and start failing in confusing ways. A scheduled sweep removes what we put there after it has posted, and tracks storage growth per device.

Trap The clear-album command is indiscriminate

The bulk clear removes everything recent, not only our uploads. Delete by name from a manifest of what we pushed, and never point the bulk clear at a device holding a client's own photos.

Module D · Proof and reporting

Proof is what makes the invoice easy to approve. It is also the cheapest trust we can buy, because the screenshots are nearly free to capture.

A screenshot of every post, as it appeared

Ready nowCore

Immediately after publishing, capture the screen and file it against the job. The client's monthly report is a wall of evidence rather than a list of claims. Full-quality device screenshots are also available through the phone helper when the client wants press-ready images.

Cost to us

Two milliseconds of rack time per capture. Storage is the only real cost.

The link to every post

Needs phone helperCore

After publishing, use the app's own share option to copy the post's link, then read it off the phone's clipboard. The report contains working links, not just pictures, which is what a client forwards to their own boss.

Cost to us

A few seconds per post, per phone.

Performance numbers from inside the app

Needs text readerAdd-on

Open the account's own insights screen and read the view, like and follower numbers off it. This is the one genuinely useful thing blocked by the missing text-recognition add-on. Until it is installed we can capture the screens but not turn them into a spreadsheet automatically.

Decision needed

Either install the add-on, or send the screenshots to an outside recognition service, or type the numbers by hand for the small number of accounts that ask. Costed in Section 8.

Screen recordings for review

Needs phone helperLater

Record the device screen during a run and pull the video back to the PC. Useful when a client disputes what happened, and useful internally when a script misbehaves in a way a still picture does not explain.

The monthly report

Needs build workCore

Assembled automatically: what posted and when, links, proof pictures, uptime against the promise, anything held for a decision, and anything we noticed about the accounts. One PDF per client per month. This document is our retention tool, so it should look as good as this one does.

Module E · Commerce operations

For clients who sell inside the phone apps. Higher price tolerance than content clients, because the work maps directly to revenue they can count.

Listing publisher for seller apps

Needs phone helperAdd-on

Photos, title, price and description go in from a spreadsheet, and the listing gets created in the seller app itself. Covers the marketplaces that have no usable desktop path.

Cost to us

Roughly 60 to 120 seconds per listing per device. This is per-listing work, so price it per listing, not per month.

Price and relist sweeps

Ready nowAdd-on

Walk the client's listings and apply a price change or a relist across the whole set. When the same change applies to many devices, it is one instruction for all of them.

Live selling control room

Needs phone helperLater

During a live stream, a second device runs the seller's control panel: rotating which product is pinned on a timer and posting prepared comments at the right moments, while the host only has to talk.

Why it is later

It needs an operator watching in real time, so it only makes sense once the Desk package has staff.

Photo round trip

Needs phone helperLater

Pull product photos the client shot on the phone back to the PC, clean them up, and push the finished versions back for listing or posting. Removes a fiddly manual step that clients do badly.

Module F · The device lab

The same rack, rented as a testing bench. This is a separate revenue line with a different buyer, and it is the segment least exposed to anything that happens on the social platforms. Worth building even if it never becomes the biggest number.

Do the links work?

Needs phone helperAdd-on

Fire every campaign link at a set of real iPhones and photograph where each one lands, with the app installed, not installed, and already open. Catches the broken link before a client spends money driving traffic to it.

Cost to us

The links go out to the whole rack in one instruction. Only the proof pictures are per device.

Did the push notification arrive, and how long did it take?

Needs outside serviceAdd-on

Watch the top strip of every screen at a high rate and timestamp the moment a notification appears. Compared against the send time, that gives real delivery latency on real devices, plus a picture of how the notification actually looked.

Email rendering on real iPhones

Needs build workAdd-on

Open the campaign email in the real iOS mail app, scroll it, capture each screenful and stitch them into one image. Marketing teams currently pay a subscription for a worse version of this that uses simulators.

App Store screenshots and language checks

Needs phone helperAdd-on

Drive an app to the screens that need to appear in a store listing, in each language, and capture them at full device resolution. Tedious by hand, quick on a rack.

Does checkout still work?

Needs outside serviceLater

Run a real purchase through the client's app on a schedule and alert when it breaks. High value, and the most sensitive thing on the list because it touches payment details, so it needs its own controls before we sell it.

Ad slots, accessibility and crash logs

Ready nowAdd-on

Three smaller lab jobs that share the same machinery: confirm ad slots actually fill and time how long they take, walk an app with the keyboard to check the focus order for accessibility, and pull crash logs off a device without needing a developer's laptop.

Module G · The parts clients never see

Sold as reliability rather than as features, but they are what the uptime promise in Section 2 is made of.

Locked front door

Needs build workCore

The control software has no password of any kind. Anyone who can reach it on the network can drive every phone we own. We put an authenticated gateway in front of it, keep the rack on its own isolated network, and never expose the control port. This is the first thing to build and it is not optional.

Also

Watch for configuration changing underneath us, since anything on that network can change it silently.

Slot registry

Ready nowCore

Every phone has a name, a group, a physical rack position and a client. Without this, finding the one broken phone in a rack of fifty means unplugging things. With it, a failure names its own slot.

Automatic reconnection

Ready nowCore

Phones drop their screen connection on their own schedule. A sweeper reconnects anything that has fallen off, and new phones connect automatically when plugged in. Without this, someone babysits the rack every morning.

Cable and board watchdog

Ready nowAdd-on

The software tells us the moment a board or cable disconnects. We reset it automatically and, if it keeps flapping, raise a ticket naming the exact slot to go and touch.

Find the phone in the rack

Needs phone helperAdd-on

Turn on the torch of the phone that needs attention so a person can walk up to the right one. Small feature, saves real time once there are more than about twenty devices in a cabinet.

More than one host PC

Needs outside servicePhase 4

One PC holds roughly fifty devices at normal quality, or around a hundred at the lowest. Past that we add another PC with its own receiver and a coordinator that treats them as one fleet. Needed at roughly sixty devices, which is where the business plan says month nine.

Remote hands

Ready nowCore

Any phone's live screen in a browser with working mouse and keyboard. This is how an operator handles anything the automation cannot, and it is also the demo that sells the service, because a prospect can watch their own account being driven in real time.

Tip The demo that closes

Live screen of a real iPhone, the prospect names a caption, it types itself and posts, and a screenshot lands in a folder. Thirty seconds, no slides. The capability audit confirms every step of that demo works with what is already on the rack, except the media upload, which needs the phone helper.

Why the boring one moved to the top

Agencies managing other companies' accounts were originally ranked last for being unexciting. They are the best first customer for four concrete reasons.

  • Authorization is already solved. They hold a signed agreement with each client that says they may operate the accounts. Our paperwork is a countersignature rather than a new conversation.
  • They pay like a business. Invoices, net 30, a card on file that will not get shut off. The service is an ordinary line item in a budget that already exists.
  • They expand on their own. Land six accounts, and every new client of theirs adds devices without a new sale.
  • They buy the proof. The monthly report is something they forward to their own client with their logo on it. That makes our reporting a retention feature rather than an overhead.

The trade is that they are slower to close, typically six to ten weeks, and they will ask about data handling. Both are manageable, and both are signs of a customer who stays.

Bringing a client on

Target five working days from signature to first scheduled post. Every step below has a failure mode that shows up later if it is skipped.

  1. Authorization, in writing, per account. Who owns each account and confirmation that the client may appoint us to operate it. Countersigned before a device is assigned.
  2. Device assignment. Phone taken from the ready pool, named, put in a group for that client, position recorded.
  3. Phone preparation. The accessibility settings the control hardware needs, screen lock off, orientation locked, automatic updates off so a platform app does not change underneath us unannounced.
  4. Sign in. The client's own credentials, entered once. Recovery and two-factor stay on the client's side, always. We never become the recovery contact.
  5. Calibration. Confirm taps land where we think they do on this exact model and system version, then save a reference set of screens for the change detector.
  6. Phone helper install. The Apple Shortcut, installed and bound by hand on that device. Currently the longest and least certain step.
  7. Test post. One real post to a quiet place, with proof, reviewed by the client before the calendar goes live.

Trap Never hold the recovery method

It is tempting to take over the recovery email or the two-factor number to make sign-ins easier. Do not. The moment we hold recovery we have effectively taken the account rather than been lent it, which changes what we are in a dispute, and it turns a client offboarding into a hostage negotiation. Keep recovery with the owner even when it costs us a support call.

Letting a client go

Offboarding is a sales feature, so make it crisp. Within two business days of notice: sign out of every device in front of the client if they want to watch, hand over the evidence archive and reports, delete their content from our storage on a stated schedule, wipe and re-image the phones, and return them to the ready pool. Send written confirmation of each step. A clean exit is what makes the next prospect comfortable, and departing clients refer.

Security and separation

Three things matter here, and the first one is urgent.

  • The control software has no password. Anything on the same network can drive every phone. The rack goes on its own isolated network segment, the control port is never exposed, and all access runs through a gateway we control that requires authentication and logs who did what.
  • One device, one client. Not for any technical reason, but because a client's content and a client's session should never share hardware with another's. It is also the simplest answer to the data question in a procurement review.
  • Credentials are handled once and stored properly. In a password manager with access limited to the operators who need it, entered at setup, not kept in job files or spreadsheets. Rotate when staff change.

Keep our own marketing accounts on entirely separate hardware and a separate network from client delivery. If something goes wrong on one side, it should not be able to reach the other.

MARK-GUARDRAILS-MARK

Section 7 · The line

What we do not do, and why that sells

The same rack can be pointed at work that is ordinary business and at work that gets accounts removed and invites legal trouble. The difference is not technical, it is who owns the accounts and whether anyone is being misled. Deciding this once, in writing, is what makes everything else in this plan financeable.

The rule

We operate accounts that our client owns or is contractually authorized to operate, and we do not make an account look like it belongs to someone other than whoever really runs it.

That single sentence decides every edge case below without a debate.

We doWe do not
Post to the client's own brand, location and creator accountsRun accounts presented as unaffiliated fans or independent third parties
Reply to people who contacted the clientSend unsolicited commercial messages to bought or scraped lists
Like and comment as the client's own account, openlyManufacture engagement from accounts that exist to inflate numbers
Stop and report when a platform says slow downWork around a limit, a block or an enforcement action
Keep each client's devices separate for confidentialityArrange devices or connections to hide that accounts are related
Label AI-operated personas as the rules requirePresent a synthetic persona as a real person
Hand accounts back cleanly on exitHold recovery methods so a client cannot leave

Why this is a commercial advantage, not a tax

  • It is the only version that survives a procurement review. Agency clients have their own contracts with their own customers. If we cannot answer the authorization question, we cannot sign the good deals at all.
  • Payments stay boring. Managed social media operations is an ordinary service category. The moment the work is about evading platform enforcement, processors and banks treat it differently, and that risk arrives without warning.
  • Our costs stay predictable. A business built on accounts that keep getting removed is really a business selling replacement accounts, which is labor we cannot schedule and cannot price. Authorized accounts do not have that failure mode.
  • It is a differentiator we can say out loud. The competitor pitch is more phones. Ours is that everything we do is something the client can explain to their own customer, and that the exit is clean.

Tip Put the line in the sales deck, not the appendix

Agencies have usually been pitched by someone whose service would embarrass them if a client asked how it worked. Leading with the rule sorts prospects fast, disarms the compliance question before it is asked, and makes the price easier to hold because we are not competing with the cheapest possible version of the work.

What to do when a prospect asks for the other thing

It will happen, and the answer should be quick and non-judgmental so the relationship survives it. Decline the specific request, name what we can do instead, and keep the door open. A lot of these prospects have legitimate accounts elsewhere in their business, and the ones who do not will often come back once they have been burned by someone who said yes.

Do not negotiate this in the moment or make case-by-case exceptions. One exception becomes the policy, and the exception is always the account that eventually causes the problem.

What goes in the contract

  1. The client owns or is authorized to operate every account, and will tell us immediately if that stops being true.
  2. The client is responsible for the content they give us and for having the rights to it.
  3. We operate accounts in line with the platforms' rules, and we will pause work rather than work around an enforcement action.
  4. Consent records for any outbound messaging are the client's responsibility, and we may refuse a list.
  5. Either side can end it with thirty days' notice, with the offboarding steps from Section 6 written out.
  6. Our data handling and retention, as in the table in Section 6.

Have a lawyer in the relevant jurisdiction review this before the first agency deal, not after the tenth. It is a few hundred dollars against a contract that will be reused a hundred times.

MARK-BUILD-MARK

Section 8 · Execution

Build sequence: the first 90 days

Five phases, each with a test that says whether it is finished. The order is chosen so that the two things most likely to kill the plan get tested in the first two weeks, while changing course is still cheap.

Phase 0 · Weeks 1-2 · Clear the unknowns

Find out whether the plan is real

Three questions block everything downstream. Answer them before writing a line of product code.

  • Does the phone helper work? Install the Apple Shortcut on one device, bind it, and prove all four things it gives us: push a photo to the camera roll, push text with emoji to the clipboard, read the clipboard back, and open a link. Without this there is no media posting, no emoji captions and no post links, which removes most of the Core feature set.
  • How will we read numbers off a screen? Price the text-recognition add-on from the vendor against sending screenshots to a cloud recognition service. Decide, because the analytics feature and several lab features depend on it.
  • What does the licence cost per device? Confirm with the vendor. It is the one cost line in Section 5 that is a guess, and it moves the floor price.

Also this phase

Put the gateway in front of the control software and move the rack onto its own network. No client account should ever touch an unauthenticated control port.

Done when

A photo taken on the PC appears in a phone's camera roll, an emoji caption pastes into a real app, and the control port is unreachable from the normal network.

Phase 1 · Weeks 2-5 · The control plane

Build the thing we actually own

The vendor software drives phones. It does not know about clients, schedules, retries or evidence. That layer is our product and nobody else has it.

  • Device registry. Name, group, rack position, client, current state for every phone.
  • Job queue and scheduler. A job knows its content, its target group, its time, its retry rule and its proof requirement.
  • Two-light health board. Control and video shown separately per device, because they fail separately.
  • Self-healing ladder. Reset, release, restart app, reconnect, reset board, reboot, then escalate to a person.
  • Evidence capture. Screenshot after every action, filed against the job automatically.
  • Remote hands view. Live screen in a browser with mouse and keyboard. Also the sales demo.

Done when

A job scheduled for 9am runs on two devices at 9am, retries by itself when one fails, files proof for both, and shows red on the board when a phone is unplugged mid-run.

Phase 2 · Weeks 4-8 · Prove it scales, then dogfood it

Ten phones, then twenty

Every capacity number in this plan assumes around fifty devices per host PC. We have only ever run two at once. This is the largest untested assumption in the business and it is cheap to test now and expensive to discover later.

  • Bring ten devices up at once and hold them for a full day. Watch picture rates, dropped connections and whether the host PC keeps up.
  • Then nineteen, which is every board we own. Record where it starts to degrade and at which video quality setting.
  • Fix the pacing and the quality policy based on what actually happens, then write the real capacity number into the pricing model.

Then run our own accounts on it

Move Node Theory's own marketing accounts onto the rack, on separate hardware from anything client-facing. We become customer zero, the demo becomes real, and the go-to-market content in Section 9 starts producing while the product finishes.

Done when

Nineteen devices run a full day unattended, and our own accounts have posted for two weeks straight with proof, with no morning queue longer than thirty minutes.

Phase 3 · Weeks 8-12 · First revenue

Sell the Post and Operate packages

  • Paperwork first. Contract, authorization form and the data table from Section 6, reviewed by a lawyer.
  • Onboarding runbook. The seven steps from Section 6, timed, so a new device is a checklist and not a project.
  • Inbox watch and the monthly report. The two features that turn a posting script into a service worth a retainer.
  • Land two or three clients, ideally one agency and one smaller account, at ten to twenty devices total.

Done when

An invoice has been paid, uptime has been measured against the promise for a full month, and the report went out without anyone assembling it by hand.

Phase 4 · Beyond 90 days · Widen

Staff the desk, open the lab, add the second PC

  • Desk package once there is an operator to staff it and enough inbox volume to justify the price.
  • Device lab as a separate line with its own landing page, because it is the revenue least exposed to platform decisions.
  • Second host PC and the coordinator at around sixty devices.
  • Commerce module when a seller client asks for it, not before. It is per-listing work and should be priced per listing.

What to build first if there is only one developer

In strict order, stopping when time runs out. Everything above the line is required to take money.

  1. Gateway and network isolation.
  2. Device registry and health board.
  3. Job queue with retries.
  4. Evidence capture.
  5. Self-healing ladder.
  6. Remote hands view.
  7. Report assembly.

Items one through four are the minimum that can honestly be sold. Five is what makes it profitable. Six is what sells it. Seven is what renews it.

Trap Do not start with the client dashboard

It is the most visible thing and the least valuable. Clients in this category do not want to log in, that is what they are paying to avoid. A monthly PDF and an email when something breaks beats a dashboard for at least the first year, and costs a fraction as much to build.

MARK-RISKS-MARK

Section 10 · Honest assessment

What would break us

Ranked by how much damage they do multiplied by how likely they are. The first three are the ones to actually worry about. Each has an early warning sign, because the point of a risk list is to notice trouble a month before it arrives rather than to feel prepared.

The three that matter

1. The phone helper does not work reliably

Highest impact

The Apple Shortcut is how photos, videos, files, emoji text and post links move between the PC and the phone. It has never been proven on our rack. If it is unreliable, or Apple changes how Shortcuts behave, we lose media posting, non-English captions and post links, which is most of what the packages promise.

Early warning

Phase 0 tells us in the first two weeks. That is precisely why it is Phase 0.

If it fails

The fallback service is text-only posting, monitoring, health and the device lab, which is a smaller but genuine business. Reprice around it rather than pretending the feature exists. Do not sell media posting to anyone until it is proven on the exact device model they are buying.

2. It does not scale past a handful of phones

High impact

Every number in Section 5 assumes roughly fifty devices on one host PC. That figure comes from the vendor, not from us. We have run two at once. If the real ceiling is fifteen, the cost per device roughly triples and the packages stop working at their current prices.

Early warning

The Phase 2 scale test at ten and then nineteen devices. Watch for picture rates falling as devices are added, which would mean they are competing for the same wireless capacity.

If it fails

More host PCs, sooner, at about $1,400 each. Annoying but survivable, and mostly a hit to margin rather than to feasibility. Discover it before quoting a hundred-device deal.

3. Apps move their buttons

Certain, ongoing

We tap fixed positions on the screen. Every app redesign moves something and breaks whatever script touched it. This is not a risk that gets resolved, it is a permanent maintenance cost, and it is the main reason this business needs staff rather than just servers.

Early warning

The nightly reference-screen comparison in Module C, which turns a surprise into a morning task.

How to live with it

Budget engineering time every week for it, keep the number of distinct screens we depend on as small as possible, and prefer the paths that do not depend on layout, such as opening a composer by link or launching apps by typing their name into search.

C. Worked example: what one device can carry

A typical Operate client wants three posts a day across four platforms, with proof.

  • Content upload to the device, once per asset: about a minute, shared across the platforms it goes to.
  • Four composer runs at roughly 45 seconds each: about three minutes.
  • Four proof screenshots and four link captures: well under a minute.
  • Total device time: roughly five minutes per posting round, fifteen minutes a day.

A device is therefore busy about 1% of the day. The constraint on a client's account is never our hardware, it is what the platform and the audience will tolerate. This is why packages are sold on post counts rather than on device hours, and why an idle device costing thirty dollars a month is not a problem worth optimizing.

E. What we are not using yet

The full capability audit covers 165 verified use cases. This plan builds on perhaps forty of them. The ones deliberately parked, with a note on when to revisit:
